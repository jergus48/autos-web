How to measure whether an automation paid for itself

23 November 2026 · 5 min read · automation, roi, planning

To see whether an automation paid for itself, compare what the task cost before (staff hours, mistakes, delays) with what it costs now, and set the difference against what you paid to build and run it. The hard part is not the maths but having honest "before" numbers, so the best time to start measuring is before anything is built.

Here is a simple method any office manager can follow with a spreadsheet.

Why is it hard to know if an automation was worth it?

Most businesses never measure the task they automate. Someone says "invoices take forever", a tool is built, and six months later nobody remembers how long "forever" was. Without a starting point, the result turns into opinion: the person who asked for it says it was great, the person who paid for it is not sure.

There is also a second problem. Time saved does not always show up as money saved. If an assistant spends two fewer hours a day on data entry but still works the same contract, the wage bill is unchanged. The value is real, but it lives in what that person does instead. You need to decide in advance what would count as a success.

What should you measure before you automate?

Pick a normal period, two to four weeks is usually enough, and track the task as it is today. Keep it light so people actually do it:

  • How often it happens. Number of invoices, orders, quote requests or reports per week.
  • How long each one takes. A rough average is fine. Ask the people who do it to time five or ten examples rather than guess.
  • Who does it. The role matters, because an hour of a finance lead costs more than an hour of a junior.
  • How often it goes wrong. Typos in amounts, orders entered twice, invoices paid late, reports with the wrong month. Note what each kind of mistake usually costs to fix.
  • How long the customer or colleague waits. For example, how many hours pass between a quote request arriving and the answer going out.

Write these down somewhere everyone can see. They become the baseline you compare against later. If you are still at the planning stage, this also makes your brief much stronger, as we explain in how to write a good brief for an automation project.

How do you put a value on the time saved?

Take the hours saved per month and multiply by a fair hourly cost for the people involved. A fair cost is more than the hourly wage: it should include employer taxes and the general overhead of having someone at a desk. Your accountant can give you a reasonable figure for your business.

Then be honest about what happens to that time. There are three common outcomes:

  1. You avoid a hire. The team handles more work without adding a person. This is the clearest saving.
  2. The time goes to more valuable work. Following up on sales, calling customers, chasing overdue payments. Harder to measure, but often worth more than the hours themselves.
  3. The time simply disappears. The task got shorter but nothing else changed. In that case the automation has made the job easier, not cheaper, and you should count it that way.

None of these is wrong, but mixing them up leads to inflated claims. If outcome three is the likely one, say so before the project starts.

What about benefits that are not hours?

Some of the best results of automation do not show up on a timesheet:

  • Fewer errors. An order typed into two systems by hand will sometimes be wrong. A connection that copies it automatically removes that whole class of mistake. Count how many errors you had before and after, and roughly what each one cost.
  • Faster answers. If quotes now go out the same day instead of after two or three, you may win work you used to lose. You cannot always prove this, but you can track how many quotes turn into orders.
  • Less dependence on one person. When a process only works because one colleague knows the spreadsheet, a holiday or a resignation becomes a risk. Removing that risk has value even if you never put a number on it.

List these separately from the hours. They help explain the decision, but keep them out of the main sum unless you have real figures.

What should you count on the cost side?

The build price is the obvious part. For a typical small business project at Swiftrix that is somewhere between 1,500 and 4,000 euros, depending on how many systems are involved. But also count:

  • Running costs, such as hosting, software subscriptions and any AI usage fees.
  • Staff time during the project, for explaining the process, testing and giving feedback.
  • Upkeep. Tools change, suppliers change their invoice layout, a form gets a new field. Someone has to adjust the automation now and then.

A rough payback period is simply the total cost divided by the monthly value. If that comes out at many years, the task was probably too small to automate, and it is better to know that up front. Our post on which office tasks are worth automating covers how to spot those cases early.

When should you check the result?

Measure again once the automation has run through a normal cycle, usually one to three months after going live. Use the same method as for the baseline: count, time a sample, note the errors. Compare the two sets of numbers side by side.

If the result is weaker than expected, look at why before deciding it failed. Often a few edge cases are still handled by hand, or the team has not fully switched over. Those are fixable.

A short checklist

  • Measure the task for a few weeks before building anything.
  • Agree what success means: an avoided hire, time moved to better work, fewer errors or faster replies.
  • Count all costs, including upkeep and your own team's time.
  • Check again after one to three months using the same method.
