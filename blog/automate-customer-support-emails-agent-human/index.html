Automating support emails: what to give an agent, what to keep

22 November 2026 · 5 min read · ai agents, customer support, email

An AI agent is a good fit for support emails that are frequent, repetitive and have a clear correct answer, such as order status, opening hours, delivery times or "where is my invoice". Anything involving money, blame, strong emotion or a judgement call (complaints, refunds, cancellations, legal threats) should stay with a person, with the agent preparing the case rather than answering it.

Most of the value, and most of the risk, comes from drawing that line in the right place for your business.

Which support emails are safe to hand to an AI agent?

Look for emails where the answer already exists somewhere and a new employee could reply correctly after a short explanation. Examples:

  • Order and delivery status. "Has my order shipped?" The answer is in your order system or the courier's tracking page.
  • Repeat questions about how things work. Opening hours, returns address, how to book, what to bring to an appointment, which file format you accept.
  • Document requests. "Can you resend my invoice?" or "I need a copy of the delivery note." The agent can find the document and attach it.
  • Simple changes with clear rules. Changing a delivery address before dispatch, moving an appointment to an open slot, updating contact details.
  • Sorting and tagging. Even when the agent does not reply, it can read every incoming email, label it (order, complaint, supplier, spam) and put it in the right queue.

What these have in common: the facts can be checked, a wrong answer is easy to spot and cheap to correct, and the customer mostly wants speed rather than empathy.

Which emails should always stay with a person?

Keep a human in charge whenever the reply could cost money, damage trust or create a commitment you did not mean to make:

  • Complaints and angry customers. People who are upset want to feel heard by a person. A polite but generic reply can make things worse.
  • Refunds, discounts and compensation. These are business decisions. An agent can gather the order details and the history, but a person should decide.
  • Cancellations of contracts or subscriptions, especially where there are notice periods or fees.
  • Anything legal or regulatory. Data access requests under GDPR, warranty disputes, threats of legal action, insurance claims.
  • Long-standing or large customers, where the relationship matters more than the speed of the reply.
  • Anything the agent is unsure about. This is the most important rule. A well-built agent should recognise when a case is unclear and pass it on rather than guess.

For these emails the agent still saves time. It can write a short summary at the top ("customer ordered on the 3rd, item arrived damaged, second complaint this year"), pull up the order and suggest a draft that a person edits and sends.

How do you decide where to draw the line?

Start by reading your inbox, not by choosing software. Take two or three weeks of real support emails and sort them into rough groups. You will usually find that a handful of question types make up a large share of the volume, and a long tail of one-off cases makes up the rest.

For each group, ask three questions:

  1. Is there a single correct answer we could write down? If two of your staff would answer differently, it is not ready for an agent.
  2. Where does the answer live? If it is in a system the agent can read (order software, booking calendar, a document folder), good. If it lives in one person's head, write it down first.
  3. What happens if the reply is wrong? A wrong opening time is a small embarrassment. A wrong refund promise is a real cost.

The groups that pass all three are your starting point. Everything else stays human for now. We go deeper on this kind of sorting in how to tell if a task is too messy to automate.

Should the agent send replies itself or only draft them?

There are three sensible levels, and many businesses move through them over time:

  • Sort only. The agent reads and labels incoming email so staff open the right queue. No customer sees anything written by the agent.
  • Draft for approval. The agent writes a reply and a person checks it and presses send. This is a safe way to learn how good the drafts really are with your own emails.
  • Send directly for chosen categories. Once drafts for, say, order status questions are consistently right, those can go out without review, while everything else still waits for a person.

Starting at draft level costs a little speed but catches problems before customers see them, and it gives your team confidence in the agent.

What can go wrong?

Being honest about the failure modes is part of designing for them:

  • Outdated information. If your returns policy changes and nobody updates the source the agent reads, it will confidently give the old answer.
  • Misreading the question. A customer asking "can I still change my order?" may really be asking to cancel. Clear handover rules matter more than clever wording.
  • Tone. Replies that are correct but cold can annoy people. It helps to give the agent examples of how your best staff write.
  • Silent failures. If a connection to the order system breaks, the agent must say so and hand over, not invent an answer. Someone should check a sample of replies regularly.

We wrote a full post on what happens when an AI agent gets something wrong and how to plan for it.

When is it not worth automating support email at all?

If you receive a few support emails a day and they are mostly different from each other, a person answering them is probably cheaper and better. The same goes for businesses where support is really sales: a personal reply from the owner may be the reason customers come back. Automation pays off when the same questions arrive again and again and someone is spending a real part of their week typing the same answers.

Where to start

Pick one or two of your most common, lowest-risk question types, let an agent draft replies for a few weeks and keep everything else with your team.
