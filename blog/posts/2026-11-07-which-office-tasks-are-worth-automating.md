A task is worth automating when it is repeated often, follows rules that rarely change, and costs your team real time each week. If one of those three is missing, the case gets weak quickly. Most offices have a handful of tasks that pass all three, and they are usually not the ones people complain about loudest.

## The three questions to ask first

**How often does it happen?** A task done fifty times a week is a candidate. A task done twice a year almost never pays back the effort of automating it, however annoying it feels on the day.

**Are the rules stable?** If a person can explain the steps in a few sentences and the steps do not change from one case to the next, a program can follow them. If the answer is "it depends, I just know", you are looking at judgement, and judgement needs more care.

**What does it cost?** Add up the minutes, not the irritation. Ten minutes, thirty times a week, is five hours. Over a year that is a working month spent on one chore.

## Tasks that usually pay off

- **Copying data between systems.** Orders from an email into your order system, a form submission into your CRM, a delivery note into the stock list. These are the classic wins because the work is pure transfer.
- **Producing the same report every week or month.** If someone builds the same spreadsheet by pulling numbers from three places, a program can pull them and format the result.
- **Sorting and routing incoming email.** Enquiries, invoices and complaints can be recognised and sent to the right person or folder.
- **Reminders and follow-ups.** Chasing unpaid invoices, confirming appointments, asking for missing documents.
- **Reading structured documents.** Invoices and delivery notes follow a pattern, so the key fields can be extracted and checked.

## Tasks that are usually not worth it

- **Rare tasks.** Year-end procedures done once rarely justify a build.
- **Tasks where the process itself is still being worked out.** Automating a process you are about to change locks in the old way of working. Settle the process first.
- **Work that depends on a relationship.** A call to an unhappy long-term customer should stay with a person. An automation can prepare the background for that call, but it should not make it.
- **Decisions with real consequences and no clear rule.** Approving an unusual discount or judging a borderline quality problem needs a human who owns the decision.

## A middle path: automate the preparation, not the decision

Many tasks sit between the two lists. Automation can do 80 percent of the work and leave the last step to a person: the program reads the invoice, fills in the fields and flags anything odd, and a clerk approves it with one click. This keeps a person in charge while removing the typing, and it is often the safest first step.

## How to pick your first one

1. List the tasks your team repeats every week. Ask the people doing them, not their managers.
2. Estimate hours per week for each one.
3. Cross out anything that fails the stability test.
4. Start with the one that has the most hours and the clearest steps.
