To automate onboarding paperwork in a small company, collect the new starter's details once through a single form, then let that information fill the contract, the payroll record, the system accounts and the first-week checklist. The welcome, the introductions and any judgement about the person should stay with people.

Most small businesses do not need an HR platform to get there. They need to stop typing the same name, address and bank details into five places.

## What does onboarding paperwork usually look like?

In a typical office, shop or workshop, a new hire sets off a chain of small tasks spread across several people:

- someone sends the contract and waits for it to come back signed
- someone asks for bank details, tax information and an emergency contact
- the details are typed into payroll, often again into a staff spreadsheet
- someone else creates an email account and logins for the tools the person will use
- a manager remembers, or forgets, to order a laptop, keys, a uniform or safety equipment
- policies are emailed as attachments and nobody records who has read them

None of this is difficult. The trouble is that it lives in people's heads and inboxes. When the office manager is on holiday, the new starter arrives to find no login and no desk.

## Which parts are worth automating?

The parts that follow the same steps every time and involve copying information from one place to another.

**One intake form.** The new starter fills in their details once, ideally before the first day. The form checks the obvious things: a valid email, a complete address, a bank account in the right format. Everything later reads from this one record.

**Documents filled from that record.** The employment contract, the job description and any standard letters can be generated from templates with the name, role, start date, salary and working hours filled in. A person still checks the result before it is sent. Signing can be digital, which saves printing and chasing paper. We wrote about this for factory staff in [replacing paper forms with digital signing](/blog/replace-paper-forms-with-digital-signing).

**Payroll and staff records.** If your payroll or accounting software accepts imports or has a connection, the details can be passed across instead of re-typed. Where it does not, the automation can at least prepare the data in the exact format your payroll provider asks for.

**Accounts and equipment.** Creating an email address, adding the person to the right groups and sending a request to whoever orders equipment can all be triggered by the same record. For tools that have no connection, the automation can create a task for the person responsible.

**A checklist that tracks itself.** Instead of a printed list, each step is ticked off as it happens, and anything still open after a set number of days goes to the manager as a reminder. This is the part people tend to value most, because nothing gets quietly forgotten.

**Policy acknowledgements.** The new starter receives the handbook and safety documents and confirms they have read them. You get a dated record without keeping a folder of signed sheets.

## What should stay personal?

The first day matters more than any form. A manager meeting the person, showing them around and explaining how things really work cannot be replaced by an email sequence, and it should not be.

Keep these with people:

- the welcome and the first conversation about the role
- introductions to the team and to key customers or suppliers
- any decision about the contract terms themselves
- anything sensitive the new starter shares, such as health needs or a request for adjustments

Automation should take care of the admin so the manager has time for these, not the other way round.

## What about legal checks and personal data?

Onboarding involves some of the most sensitive data a company holds: identity documents, bank details, sometimes health information. That raises the bar.

Some checks have to be done properly by a person. In the UK, for example, employers must carry out right to work checks, and the rules on how they are done and recorded are specific. An automation can remind you, store the result and track the deadline, but it should not decide on its own that a document is valid.

On data protection, a few points apply to any automation that touches staff records:

- collect only what you actually need, and only when you need it
- limit who can see bank details and identity documents
- know which outside services the data passes through, and have agreements in place with them
- decide how long you keep records for people who did not start or who have left

## When is it not worth automating?

If you hire two people a year, the honest answer is probably a good checklist template and a shared folder. Building and maintaining an automation for something that happens twice a year rarely pays back, and the steps will have changed by the next hire anyway.

It is also worth waiting if your process is not settled. If every manager onboards differently, agree on one way first. Automating an unclear process just produces unclear results faster.

The case gets stronger when you hire regularly, take on seasonal or temporary staff, run several sites, or have new people who need many system accounts and safety sign-offs before they can start work.

## How do you start?

Write down every step that happens between "offer accepted" and "fully set up", who does each step, and which system it touches. Most companies are surprised by how long the list is. Then mark the steps where information is copied from one place to another. Those are the first candidates.

A sensible first project is usually the intake form plus the filled-in contract and the self-tracking checklist. Payroll connections and account creation can follow once the basic flow works and your team trusts it.
