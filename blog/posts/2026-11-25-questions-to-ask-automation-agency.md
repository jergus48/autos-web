Before you hire an automation agency, ask what exactly is included in the price, what happens when the automation makes a mistake, and who owns the result. If the answers are specific to your process, that is a good sign; if they would fit any company, keep looking.

We are a small automation studio ourselves, so we have an interest here. The questions below are ones you should put to us as well.

## Questions about how well they understand the work

### 1. Can you describe our process back to us?

Do not ask about technology first. Explain the task, for example how an order travels from a customer email to your stock system, and ask them to repeat it in their own words. Someone who has understood will mention the awkward parts: the customer who writes product names wrong, the order that arrives as a photo, the colleague who has to approve large amounts.

If they move straight to a tool name, they may be fitting your problem to something they already sell.

### 2. Which part of this would you not automate?

Almost every process has a step that should stay with a person: a judgement call, a sensitive customer, a case that happens twice a year. An honest supplier can point to it. Someone who says everything can be automated has either not looked closely or is hoping you will not.

### 3. Can we see something you have built that works?

A live demo or a screen share tells you more than a list of logos. It does not have to come from your industry. Ask what went wrong during that project and how they dealt with it. A supplier with real experience will have an answer.

Some client work is confidential, so it is fair if they cannot show everything. They should still be able to show you something.

## Questions about price and scope

### 4. What is included, and what costs extra?

Ask for the scope in writing: which inputs, which systems, which cases, how many rounds of changes, and whether testing and training your staff are included. A price without a scope means little, because each side will picture a different project.

Also ask what happens when you request something new halfway through. Changes are normal. What you want is an agreed way to price them before the work is done.

### 5. What will we pay every month afterwards?

The build is one cost. Running it is another. There may be hosting, licences for connector tools, charges for AI models that depend on volume, and a support fee. Ask for an estimate based on your own monthly volume, and ask what happens to the bill if that volume doubles.

### 6. How long will it take, and what do you need from us?

Projects often wait on the client: access to a system, sample documents, a decision about an exception. A good supplier will tell you early what they need and who on your side should be available. Be cautious about a firm delivery date from someone who has not seen your data yet.

## Questions about mistakes and risk

### 7. What happens when the automation gets something wrong?

This may be the most useful question on the list. Listen for concrete things:

- how an unclear case is recognised and passed to a person
- who receives an alert when something stops working
- whether there is a log showing what was done and when
- whether an action can be undone, and how

"It will not make mistakes" is the wrong answer, particularly where an AI agent is involved. We explain the design side in [what happens when an AI agent gets it wrong](/blog/when-an-ai-agent-gets-it-wrong).

### 8. What data will you access, and where will it go?

Ask which systems they need to log into, whether they can use limited accounts instead of an administrator login, and which outside services will receive your data. If personal data is involved and you are in the EU or the UK, ask about a data processing agreement and where the data is stored. You should also know how access will be removed when the project ends.

## Questions about what happens later

### 9. Who owns the result, and can someone else take it over?

Get a clear answer on ownership of the code, the configuration and the accounts. Ideally the accounts are in your company's name from the start, and the handover includes short documentation. Not every arrangement works this way: some suppliers run the automation as a service, and you are paying for use and not for ownership. That can be fine, as long as you know it before you sign and know what you would keep if you left.

### 10. Who fixes it when something changes?

Other systems change. A supplier redesigns an invoice, a software vendor updates a connection, a colleague renames a column. Ask who notices, how quickly they respond, and what it costs. If the agency is one or two people, ask what happens when they are ill or away. Small suppliers can be a good choice, but they should have thought about this.

## Warning signs

- A guaranteed percentage saving before anyone has looked at your process.
- No questions about exceptions, volumes or the people who do the task today.
- Pressure to sign this week.
- A large first project with no smaller step to test the relationship.
- Nothing in writing about scope, ownership or support.

One of these on its own may just be clumsy selling. Several together are a reason to stop.

## When you do not need an agency at all

If the task takes someone a few minutes a week, or a feature in software you already pay for would handle it, hiring anyone is probably a poor use of money. The same is true if the process changes every month: settle it first, then automate. A decent supplier should tell you so.

## How to compare the answers

Put the same ten questions to two or three suppliers and write the answers side by side. Give more weight to the answers on mistakes, ownership and support than to the presentation. Then start with one contained task, agree how you will judge it, and decide about the next one when you have seen the first one running.
