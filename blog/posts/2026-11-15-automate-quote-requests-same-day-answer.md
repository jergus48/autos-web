You can answer most quote requests the same day by automating the steps around the price, not the price itself: collecting the missing details, looking up standard items and preparing a draft for a person to approve. The pricing decision usually takes minutes. It is the waiting, chasing and re-typing around it that takes days.

## Why do quotes take so long in the first place?

Follow one request through a typical small business and the delay is rarely where people expect it.

A request arrives by email or through a website form. It sits in a shared inbox until someone has time. That person reads it and finds that something is missing: a quantity, a size, a delivery address, a drawing. They write back and wait. When the answer comes, they open the price list, check stock or a supplier's price, type the lines into a quote template, save it as a PDF and send it. Then the request is forgotten unless the customer replies.

The actual judgement (what should this cost, and do we want the job) is a small part of that chain. The rest is routine, and routine is what software handles well.

## Which parts of quoting can be automated?

Four parts, in rough order of how easy they are.

**Capturing the request.** Every request, whatever the channel, lands in one list with the customer, the date and the original message. Nothing depends on someone remembering to forward an email.

**Asking for what is missing.** This is often the biggest time saver. If your quotes always need the same five facts, an AI agent can read the incoming message, see which of them are there and reply within minutes asking for the rest. The customer gets a quick, specific answer instead of silence, and by the time a person looks at the request it is complete.

**Preparing the draft.** For items with known prices, the system fills in the quote: lines, quantities, prices from your list, your standard terms. For anything it cannot price, it leaves the line open and flags it.

**Following up.** A polite reminder after a few days if the customer has not answered, and a note to you when they do.

We described the reading part in more detail in the post on [order entry from customer emails](/blog/automate-order-entry-from-customer-emails). Quote requests are the same problem one step earlier in the sale.

## Should the quote go out without a person seeing it?

For most businesses, no, at least not at the start.

A sensible split looks like this. Standard requests (catalogue items, normal quantities, existing customers) are drafted in full and wait for one click of approval. Anything unusual goes to a person with the facts already collected: custom work, very large orders, new customers asking for credit, anything where the agent was not sure what was meant.

A wrong price sent to a customer is awkward to take back, so approval is cheap insurance. After a few weeks you will see which kinds of quote you approve without ever changing them. Those are the candidates for sending automatically, if you want to go that far. Many businesses decide they do not, and that is a reasonable choice.

## What do you need to have in place first?

Automation cannot invent a pricing logic you do not have. Before building anything, check three things.

- **A price list that is actually current.** If the real prices live in one person's head, or in last year's spreadsheet with corrections made from memory, fix that first. It is dull work and it pays off even without automation.
- **A list of the facts every quote needs.** Write down what you ask customers again and again. That list becomes the agent's checklist.
- **Clear rules for exceptions.** Who approves discounts? Above what order size does the owner want to see the quote? When is the answer "we do not do that"? If two colleagues would answer differently, the software will not settle it for you.

Getting these three written down is often half the project.

## When is automating quotes not worth it?

Be honest about volume and variety.

If you send a handful of quotes a month, a good template and a habit of answering within the day will do more than any system. The build would cost more than the time it saves.

If every job is truly one of a kind (a bespoke renovation, a complex engineering project), the price comes from a site visit and an experienced estimate. Software can still collect the request details and keep track of follow-ups, but it should not try to produce the number.

And if your quotes are slow because prices from suppliers arrive late, the problem sits outside your office. Automating your side will make the delay more visible, but it will not remove it.

The good cases are in the middle: a steady flow of requests, mostly built from known items and rules, with a minority of special cases. Trade suppliers, print shops, workshops, transport firms and service companies with a rate card often fit.

## How to start small

Do not begin with the full chain. Pick the step that hurts most.

For many teams that is the first reply. Getting every request acknowledged quickly, with the missing questions asked, shortens the whole process even if the quote itself is still typed by hand. Once that works, add the draft for your most common request type, then the reminders.

Keep a simple count before and after: how many requests came in, and how many were answered the same day. It tells you whether the change did anything, and it is easy to collect.
