Five signs your team spends too much time on data entry

18 November 2026 · 5 min read · data entry, automation, office work, productivity

The clearest signs are that the same information gets typed into more than one place, that small errors keep reaching customers, and that work piles up whenever one particular person is away. If you recognise two or more of the signs below, it is worth measuring how much time the typing really takes before deciding whether to automate any of it.

Data entry rarely looks like a problem. It is not a line in the budget and the people doing it are usually quick and careful, so it grows quietly.

Sign 1: the same information is typed more than once

Follow one customer order through your business. In many offices it arrives by email, gets typed into a spreadsheet, then into the stock system, then into the accounting software when the invoice goes out. Each step is a person reading one screen and typing into another.

The giveaway is a sentence like "I just copy it over from the other system". Every copy is time spent and a chance to get a digit wrong. If a supplier name, a VAT number or a delivery address exists in three tools and all three were typed by hand, that is the first thing to look at.

This is often the easiest kind of data entry to remove, because the information already exists in digital form. It only needs to be passed along. We covered the practical options in connecting your CRM, email and accounting tool.

Sign 2: small errors keep reaching customers

A wrong quantity on a delivery note. An invoice sent to an old address. A price from last year's list. Each mistake on its own is minor and gets fixed with an apology, but if they happen every week, they point to manual typing under time pressure.

People are not the problem here. Anyone re-typing figures for hours will make occasional slips, however careful they are. The fix is not to ask people to concentrate harder, it is to reduce the number of times a figure is typed at all.

A useful habit is to keep a short list of every error a customer reports for a month, with a note of where it came from. If most of them trace back to a copying step, you have found your target.

Sign 3: there is a rush at the end of every month

If your finance or admin team stays late in the last days of each month, ask what they are doing. Often it is catching up on entries that should have been made as they happened: supplier invoices sitting in an inbox, receipts in a drawer, timesheets that still need typing into payroll.

End-of-month peaks are a sign that data entry is being batched because there is no time to do it daily. The work is the same either way, but the batching adds stress and delays reports that managers need.

This is also where the cost becomes visible. A report that should be ready on the second working day arrives on the eighth, and decisions wait for it. If that sounds familiar, our post on automating monthly reports goes through the steps.

Sign 4: one person is the only one who knows how it works

In many small companies there is one person who knows which spreadsheet feeds which system, which customers need a special code and which supplier sends invoices in a strange format. When they go on holiday, the work stops or someone else does it slowly and makes mistakes.

That is a data entry problem disguised as a staffing problem. The process lives in a person's head instead of in a system. Writing it down is the first step, and doing so often shows which parts are pure copying (easy to automate) and which parts need real judgement (keep them with a person).

Sign 5: skilled people spend their day typing

Look at who does the entry. If an engineer types measurements into a report template, if a nurse copies appointment details between two systems, or if a sales manager spends Friday afternoon updating a pipeline spreadsheet, you are paying skilled rates for clerical work.

This sign is often missed because the typing is spread thinly across many people, yet it adds up and pulls them away from the work they were hired for.

How do you measure the time it really takes?

Guessing is unreliable, so measure for two weeks. Ask each person who does data entry to keep a simple log:

  • what they typed (orders, invoices, timesheets, reports)
  • where it came from and where it went
  • roughly how long it took
  • how many corrections they had to make

Two weeks is usually enough to show a pattern without becoming a burden. At the end, add up the hours per task and multiply by a realistic hourly cost. That gives you a number you can compare against the price of fixing it, rather than a feeling that "it takes ages".

When is data entry not worth automating?

Not every bit of typing should go. Be honest about the cases where it is better left alone:

  • Low volume. If something is entered ten times a month and takes two minutes each, there is little to save.
  • Constantly changing rules. If the format or the process changes every few weeks, an automation will need fixing as often as it helps.
  • Judgement in every case. If each entry needs a person to decide something, an automation can at best prepare the data for them.
  • The source is chaotic. Handwritten notes with no fixed layout or emails that say something different every time are hard to read reliably. An AI agent can sometimes help, but expect it to hand unclear cases to a person.

We wrote more about this in how to tell if a task is too messy to automate.

What to do next

Pick the one task from your log that takes the most hours and has the clearest rules. That is usually where the first improvement pays off fastest. Sometimes the answer is a built-in integration you already pay for. Sometimes it is a small custom tool or an AI agent that reads incoming documents and enters them for review.
